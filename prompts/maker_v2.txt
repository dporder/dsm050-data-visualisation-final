The question you are answering is: {question}

The three types come from end-user programming research (Nardi 1993; Ko et al. 2011;
Scaffidi, Shaw and Myers 2005).
- Professional developer: programming is their job or their training.
- Technical but not a professional developer: comfortable with tools and code-adjacent
  work (web, IT, UX, data, automation), not a developer by trade.
- Non-programmer: programming is only a means to their own end, and no coding background
  is evident.

Rules, in order.
1. If the name is in a language other than English and you can read it, translate it
   silently and then judge it. Read the page title, when shown, as evidence equal to the name.
2. Choose the professional developer option only when the page title or the name explicitly
   describes the maker as a developer, engineer, programmer, full-stack or software professional
   (for example a portfolio titled "Developer & Creator").
3. Choose the technical but not professional option when the words name a web development,
   tech, IT or UX design agency, solutions business or practice (a web agency, tech solutions,
   a UX canvas) without describing the maker as a developer. A digital marketing, advertising
   or paid-traffic business is not technical in this sense.
4. Choose the non-programmer option only when the words themselves say so, for example a
   title that describes its maker as someone who does not code.
5. Export and builder artefacts are not evidence of anything: "-main", "-repo", "version1",
   "clone", "test", "demo", "remix-of", numeric suffixes, and a framework, library or model
   name inside a product name. Never read them as professional markers.
6. Otherwise choose the cannot tell option. A bare product, business or personal name is not
   evidence of who made it. A careful human would rather say nothing than guess, and so
   should you.
